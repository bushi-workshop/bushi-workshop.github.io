Overview

About the Workshop

Humanoid robots are moving from laboratory settings toward deployment in homes, schools, healthcare environments, public services, and care institutions. As this transition accelerates, a central question becomes increasingly important: who are humanoids designed for?

BU-SHI aims to broaden the discussion beyond default-user assumptions and toward socially deployable humanoid systems that account for diverse users, communication styles, languages, cultures, and physical or cognitive conditions.

Rather than focusing on a single communication modality, the workshop brings together perspectives on speech-based, multimodal, embodied, and visually grounded communication systems, including sign-language-based interaction.

The goal is to develop a cross-disciplinary roadmap connecting robotics, AI, language research, HRI, and real-world deployment studies.

Carlos T. Ishi

RIKEN / ATR

Humanoid system perspective.

Invited Talk Title

Social expression technologies and applications for humanoid robots

Abstract

In this talk, I will introduce our research activities on analyses of multimodal expressions including speech and non-verbal behaviors in dialogue interactions, and their applications for social robots, including desktop humanoids and android robots. Analysis results on the relationships between speech and motion features, and evaluation results on the effects of several modalities including facial expressions, eye-gazing, head and body gestures on the robot's impressions will be presented. Some recent results on situation- and user-adapted behaviors will also be presented with some perspectives on future research directions.

Mayumi Bono

National Institute of Informatics

Language and interaction perspective.

Invited Talk Title

Beyond Recognition: A Participant-Oriented Approach to Human Interaction for Adaptive Humanoid AI

Abstract

As humanoid robots increasingly enter homes, schools, healthcare, and public environments, multimodal AI has made remarkable progress in recognizing speech, gestures, gaze, facial expressions, and body movements. Yet a fundamental question remains: What should a robot actually recognize? Current multimodal AI generally assumes that human behaviour can be represented by predefined labels serving as ground truth. From the perspective of Conversation Analysis, however, meaningful action is not inherent in observable behaviour itself but is accomplished through participants' orientations, sequential organization, and publicly displayed understanding during interaction. The central challenge for adaptive humanoid AI is therefore not simply improving behavioural recognition, but understanding how people collaboratively construct meaning. In this talk, I introduce Relevant Annotation, an interactionally grounded annotation framework that reconceptualizes annotation as a participant-oriented descriptive practice rather than the assignment of observer-defined behavioural categories. Relevant Annotation distinguishes observable bodily movement from participant-relevant action, documenting how embodied conduct becomes meaningful through sequential context, recipient design, and multimodal coordination. I illustrate this perspective through two contrasting interactional cases. While current multimodal large language models successfully interpret actions whose meanings are locally recoverable from visible form, they struggle when understanding depends on participants' ongoing organization of interaction. These cases suggest that what AI currently treats as ground truth often differs fundamentally from what participants themselves demonstrably understand. I then extend this discussion to sign language interaction, arguing that sign language offers more than an accessibility application for robotics. It challenges several assumptions inherited from spoken-language research, including discrete turn exchange, stable shared lexical representations, and the view that meaning resides in individual linguistic forms. Drawing on studies of cooperative action in Japanese Sign Language, I show how participants collaboratively construct turns, negotiate reference, and even establish lexical meaning within the unfolding interaction itself. Rather than retrieving fixed representations from an internal lexicon, signers continuously adapt their embodied actions to one another, making meaning a jointly accomplished achievement rather than an individual cognitive product. Finally, I present how these ideas are being developed through interdisciplinary projects including the Japanese Sign Language Dialogue Corpus, multimodal annotation, open-vocabulary sign language recognition, and international collaborations supported by JST ASPIRE, the UK–Japan British Council programme, and the Mitsubishi Foundation. I argue that designing humanoid robots for diverse users ultimately requires moving beyond behaviour recognition toward computational models grounded in participants' own organization of interaction.

Inclusion

Accessibility & Inclusion

This workshop is committed to fostering an inclusive, accessible, and respectful research environment aligned with the values of the IEEE RO-MAN community.

Accessibility and diversity are treated not only as logistical considerations, but as foundational design principles for socially deployable humanoids.

  • Hybrid attendance will be supported where feasible.
  • Automatic live captioning will be provided via Zoom or equivalent tools.
  • Diversity among invited speakers and panelists will be actively considered.
  • We welcome interdisciplinary and cross-community perspectives.
